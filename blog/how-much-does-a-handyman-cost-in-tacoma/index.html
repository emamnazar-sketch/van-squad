How much does a handyman cost in Tacoma?

The short answer. In Tacoma, HomeBlue puts a handyman at $50 to $90 an hour, with 4 hours at $190 to $360 plus materials. BIG WORKS puts the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro’s modeled billed rate at $65 to $90 an hour. A Tacoma-area operator, Magenta Home Services, prices most work at $75 to $125 an hour, and one Tacoma pro on Thumbtack charges $90 an hour with a 4-hour minimum. The minimum charge decides what one small fix really costs.

The guides split for a plain reason. HomeBlue’s band is a city average across crews. Magenta’s band is one established operator’s own hourly price. Both can be true on the same street in East Tacoma. Ask which band your crew is in before the visit, not after.

This is where people overpay in Tacoma. They pay a 4-hour minimum for a 40-minute job because they booked one fix at a time. The Thumbtack pro above says it on his page: build a list to fill the time. A door sweep, a sticking door, and a drywall patch in one visit share one minimum. Three visits pay it three times.

Why do Tacoma’s wet winters fill a handyman list?

Tacoma’s older homes take on water in the same few places every winter. Local water-damage crews name clogged gutters, mossy roofs, and old window flashing letting water into walls, plus crawlspaces that take on water in heavy rain. Much of Hilltop and Central Tacoma was built in the early 1900s, and South Tacoma is full of mid-century homes. Those houses ask for small work, often.

Caulking opens up around tubs and windows. Flashing lifts. A gutter bracket loosens, then the gutter sags under the next storm. None of that needs a remodel crew. It needs a handyman visit in November, before a loose bracket becomes a pulled-down gutter and a wet wall. Walk your home in 98404, 98405, or 98409 this week and write down what you see. That walk is your list.

What can a handyman legally do in Washington?

Washington has no exam-based handyman license. The state requires contractor registration with the Department of Labor & Industries (L&I) before a contractor bids, advertises, or does paid work — with no small-job threshold that lets most paid work skip it, per Housecall Pro’s Washington guide built on L&I and RCW 18.27.

Registration rests on paperwork, not a test: a business registration and UBI, liability insurance, and a surety bond — $30,000 for a general contractor and $15,000 for a specialty contractor since July 1, 2024. The handyman specialty is narrow: one person doing all the work personally, no employees, minor repairs to existing homes, under $2,000 per job, and nothing that needs a building permit. On jobs over $1,000, the customer gets a signed disclosure about the bond and lien rights. Electrical and plumbing work carry their own licensing on top of registration. Ask for the registration number — it has to appear on advertising, bids, and estimates — and check it before you book.
